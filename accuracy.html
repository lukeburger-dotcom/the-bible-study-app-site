Across 222 real studies from October 10 to 17, 2026, 388 of 389 Bible passages (99.7%) were printed from the Bible text itself, 9 of the 10 passages the AI typed out were replaced with a link to the passage or removed before anyone read them (the other one matched the text word for word), and 202 of the 216 cited works looked up in library catalogs (93.5%) were found there.

How each check works

THE SCRIPTURE GATE
The AI is told to write only the reference, like John 1:1. The app looks the verse up and prints the published words itself. Six full Bibles are stored inside the app: the King James Version, the Berean Standard Bible, the World English Bible, the American Standard Version, Young's Literal Translation and the Darby Translation. Eight more come from their publishers with the copyright line attached: NASB 2020, the New Living Translation, the New King James Version, The Message, the Christian Standard Bible, the Amplified Bible, the Good News Translation and the Contemporary English Version. When the AI types a passage out anyway, plain code catches it before the study reaches the reader: it is replaced with a link to the passage, removed when it has no reference or its reference does not resolve, or shown only when its words match a stored Bible exactly. The gate is deterministic: the same study gets the same answer every time, because it is compared against the stored text itself. Under any quote, tap "Check this passage" to read the verse on its own.
THE WORD CARDS
When a study digs into a Greek, Hebrew or Aramaic word, the AI writes down only the verse and the word. The app builds the rest of the card from dictionaries stored inside it: the original script, how to say it, which language it is, what it means, and which dictionary each meaning comes from. Whatever the AI typed inside that card is thrown away unread. If the word is not actually in the verse, the whole card is dropped rather than shown as a guess. Those dictionaries hold 447,384 tagged words and 40,367 entries. Some of the Old Testament is Aramaic rather than Hebrew (Daniel 2:4 to 7:28, Ezra 4:8 to 6:18, Ezra 7:12 to 26, and Jeremiah 10:11), and the app calls those 4,827 words Aramaic because it reads the grammar tag on each word.
THE CITATION CHECK
Books cited in a study are matched against a shelf of 466 reference works, each confirmed to exist in a public library catalog through OpenLibrary and OpenAlex. Anything cited from off that shelf is looked up in those catalogs when the study finishes. A work the catalogs cannot confirm costs the study its line saying its citations were cross-referenced. Where a study has a source list, each book its text cites is matched against that list, and each entry in the list against the text. A cited book missing from the list costs the study its cross-referenced line, and an entry nothing cites is only logged. From October 10 to 17, 2026 that happened in 2 studies: one pointed to 7 sources its list did not hold, and one listed 2 works its text did not cite. This is a strong check, and its reach is exact: it confirms that a book exists, by author, title and year. Whether the book says what the study says it does is a different question, covered below.
THE CORE-BELIEFS INSTRUCTION
Before every study, the AI reads the app's core-beliefs instruction. On eight questions where the historic church gives one answer (the resurrection, the reliability of Scripture, that God is, who Jesus is, the gospel, the virgin birth, the deity of Christ, and salvation by grace through faith), it is told to give that answer as its own, with its evidence, and it may explain another view in order to answer it. This is an instruction to the AI, not a code check: nothing yet reads finished studies for breaks of it. The audit rounds below are how it is tested today.
None of this is the AI checking its own homework. It is ordinary code comparing what the AI wrote against something fixed: the stored Bible text, the stored dictionaries, the library catalog. Think of a spellchecker: it does not ask the writer whether their spelling looks fine; it compares every word against a dictionary. This is that, for Scripture, for original-language words, and for books.

Compared to what

When researchers have gone back to published work and checked its citations one by one, about one in seven did not support the claim attached to it. That is no mark against the people who wrote it: checking every citation by hand takes time few writers or readers have, and most of what any of us reads has not been checked that way. Here is a tool that checks the citations in every study as it finishes.

of 2,648 authors, shown a real citation of their own work, said it did not support the claim attached to it. The rate held across every field.

We are not claiming a machine reads better than a scholar does. The claim is smaller, and you can check it: this app looks up its citations in every study, and its record is above.

A lab test of the book check

The record above is what real studies got. This is a lab test, run to find the citation check's limits. In August 2026 we made 300 fake book citations, ran them through the checking pipeline beside 30 real ones, and recorded what happened to each. Every square below is one fake.

The book does not exist at all105 tested · 105 caught

A real scholar's name on a book that was not written: the kind that would truly mislead a reader. The check caught all 105.

The book is real, with the wrong person attached45 tested · 44 caught · 1 accepted

The one accepted turned out to be true: the editor we had invented for a reference work really did edit its newest edition.

The book is real, with an invented volume or book number105 tested · 90 caught · 15 accepted

Each of the 15 points at a real book, by the real author, on the real subject, with a wrong volume or section number on it. A reader who follows one lands on the right shelf at the wrong door: a flaw worth fixing, and a different thing from a source that does not exist.

A real book with an invented subtitle45 · this method cannot test them

Library catalogs confirm an author, a title and a year; a subtitle riding on a real title is beyond what they settle today, so this class is reported as unmeasured. Matching each book's barcode number would close it, and that work is not done yet.

Caught Accepted Cannot be tested this way

Of the 255 fakes this method can test, 239 were caught and 16 accepted: about 6 in 100, with a statistical ceiling near 9 in 100 at this sample size. 15 of the 16 accepted were real books carrying an invented volume or section number, and the 16th turned out to be true; all 105 invented books were caught; and all 30 real books came through, so the check does not pass the test by rejecting everything.

A rival AI reads studies line by line

Some things have no database to check them against: no catalog can say whether a particular scholar holds a particular view. So a competing AI model, with web search, reads finished studies claim by claim, under instructions to assume everything is wrong and try to prove it. The largest run, in August 2026, put 55 fixed studies through three reading packs.

Where the checking stops

  • Whether the verse fits the point. The words of a quote come from the text. Whether that verse supports the argument built around it is judgment, and the rival AI reading is pointed at it.
  • Greek and Hebrew words inside ordinary paragraphs. The word cards are built from the dictionaries. A word mentioned in passing in a paragraph is not yet tied to a dictionary entry; tying prose words to the dictionary is being built.
  • Which edition of a book. The app cites author, book and year, the level a library catalog can confirm. Page and volume numbers are allowed where the AI is confident of them, nothing verifies them, and a study carrying one has to say so under its sources. The lab test shows where this costs: invented details on real books.
  • Whether a scholar really said it. Of the 466 books on the shelf, 23 are old enough that their full text is free to read, so those are the only ones a machine could check against the book itself. For the rest, the book is confirmed real, and the view attributed to it rests on the writing rules and the audit rounds.
  • Interpretation itself. Studies present a disputed reading as disputed and name who holds each view. A database deciding whose reading of John 6 is correct would be worse than naming the limit.
  • Scripture quoted inside a paragraph. A passage set apart as a quotation is printed from the text or checked against it. A few words quoted inside a sentence are compared with the passages the study printed and with the app's other public-domain Bibles only to be counted: nothing is corrected or held back, and a misquote that matches no Bible is not caught yet. From October 10 to 17, 2026, 26 such quotations, in 21 studies, matched the wording of a different Bible from the one the study printed, without naming it.
  • Core beliefs. The core-beliefs instruction tells the AI what to say; no code check reads finished studies for breaks of it yet, so drift from it is what the audit rounds are for.
  • Two words in Genesis 31:47. Jegar-sahadutha is Aramaic, and the grammar data the app is built on labels those two words Hebrew. The app corrects exactly those two words, and we name the exception here rather than invent a rule that guesses.

What we claim, and what we do not

What we claim: Bible passages are printed by the app from the Bible text, and a passage the AI types itself is replaced with a link to the passage, removed, or shown only when it matches the text word for word; the record above counts each case. Word cards are built from dictionaries stored in the app. Cited works off the shelf are looked up in library catalogs, and a study whose citation is not found loses its cross-referenced line. The checks write down what they find in every study, and the record above is counted from what they wrote down.

What we do not claim: perfection, or any percentage we cannot show the working for. AI writing is a starting point for study. The app says so on every study, and the primary sources come first.
